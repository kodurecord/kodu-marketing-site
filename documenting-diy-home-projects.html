Homeowners · DIY

Documenting DIY home projects.

DIY work is often the most personalized and valuable improvement in a home, and the most invisible at sale. The small practice of documenting your own work — the same way a contractor would — is what makes it count.

Category Projects
Reading time 6 min
Last reviewed May 2026

You did the work. You know it was done well. The next person to read this home — a buyer, an inspector, an insurance adjuster, the next owner ten years from now — will know nothing about it unless you wrote it down.

Contractors document their work because they're required to and because their reputation depends on it. The same record, made by a homeowner about their own work, carries the same weight. It is not less credible because no one billed for it.

What a DIY project record contains

The same elements a contractor record would, scaled to the size of the project.

For any DIY project

What to capture, large or small

Project scope and date range.What you set out to do, and when. Including a finish date, even if the project ran long.
Photographs throughout.Before, during (especially with anything open), after. Behind-the-wall photos are the most valuable record of any DIY project.
Materials used, with sources.Brand and product line, where purchased, receipts where significant. Especially for materials with warranties or known performance differences.
Permits, if applicable.DIY work that requires a permit still requires a permit. Pull it, document it, schedule the inspection.
A short narrative.Two or three paragraphs. What you did, in what order, what you'd do differently. This is the document the next owner will read.
Cost, including your own time if you want to track it.For your own purposes mostly. Useful for comparing to contractor quotes on similar work later.

The narrative matters more than you'd think

The single most underrated element of a DIY project record is the narrative. A short, plain account of what you did, written within a week of completion, while you still remember. Not for marketing reasons. Not for sale reasons. Because in seven years, you will not remember the order you did things in, the trick that finally worked on the difficult corner, or the small decision you made that you'd want the next person to know about.

The narrative is the part contractors don't usually write. It's the part DIY homeowners can do uniquely well.

The story of the work is the part that disappears first. Write it down while you can still remember why you made each decision.

The photographs that matter most

For any project that involves opening a wall, ceiling, floor, or any enclosed cavity: photograph it. Multiple angles. With a phone, in whatever lighting you have. These photographs become more valuable every year they exist, and they cannot be recreated after the cavity is closed.

  • Plumbing rough-in before the wall closes. Where the lines run, what kind of pipe, what you tied into.
  • Electrical rough-in. Wire runs, the boxes, the connections.
  • Anything you patched, repaired, or replaced behind a finished surface.
  • Any unexpected condition you found. (Most DIY projects reveal at least one. Photograph what you found before you fixed it.)
  • Any code-relevant detail. Bracing, fire-blocking, ventilation, fastener type and spacing.

Permits, and the case for pulling them

A common DIY thought process: "It's a small job. I know what I'm doing. I don't need a permit." Sometimes accurate. Sometimes not. The case for pulling permits on permittable DIY work, even when no one would notice if you didn't:

  • The permit is a record. The inspection is a verification. Both add credibility to your work that a homeowner's own claim cannot match.
  • Unpermitted work, when discovered at sale, becomes a negotiation point. Permitted DIY work is invisible at sale.
  • Insurance claims on damage related to unpermitted work can be denied. Permitted work doesn't have this exposure.
  • The inspector frequently catches things you didn't. This is usually a feature, not a bug.

The case against is usually time and modest fees. For most DIY projects of any consequence, the time and fees are dramatically outweighed by the record they produce.

How DIY work reads at sale

Buyers are appropriately skeptical of DIY work, because most DIY work is undocumented. The documentation is what changes the read. A buyer who sees photographs from the renovation, a permit and inspection record, a clear list of materials, and a narrative explaining the work approaches the same DIY kitchen differently than one who sees a kitchen they have to trust.

The work is the same. The buyer's interpretation isn't. A documented DIY kitchen is a renovated kitchen. An undocumented DIY kitchen is a question mark.

The smallest possible practice

For homeowners who don't want to maintain an elaborate project log: the smallest version is a single text file per project, with photographs, written within a week of completion. That's enough. The act of writing it down is most of the value. The structure is small.

The most experienced DIY homeowners write less, not more, and the records are still excellent. They write the things that won't be obvious to anyone who didn't do the work themselves.
