Roof replacement records: what to keep and why.

A new roof is one of the largest investments most homeowners ever make in a single line item. The warranty is among the most valuable they will ever hold. Both are commonly undocumented within five years of installation.

Category Systems
Reading time 8 min
Last reviewed May 2026

Your roof has a brand, a date, a warranty, a contractor, and a story. Most homeowners cannot name any of it five years after installation. This page is what to do about that.

A roof's records are unusual among home systems in one important way: when something goes wrong, you almost certainly will not be able to recreate them. A leak is not the time to discover the warranty was conditional on annual inspections that you didn't know existed. The records to keep are the records that protect the value of work already paid for.

What a complete roof record contains

The shape of a complete roof record is small. Six categories, most of which you receive at installation if you ask for them.

Roof — the records that matter

What every homeowner should have, after a roof replacement

Installation date and contractor.The full company name, license number, lead installer, and contact information. Not just the business card.
Material specification.Brand and product line of the shingles (or other roofing material), color, weight, and any underlayment or ice-and-water shield used.
The warranty document itself.Most major roofing materials carry both a manufacturer warranty and a workmanship warranty. They are different. Keep both.
Warranty registration confirmation.Most manufacturer warranties require registration within a window after installation. The confirmation email is the proof.
The permit and final inspection.Filed with the county or municipality. The final inspection is what your insurer and the next buyer's inspector will want to see.
Photographs of the work.The deck before underlayment. The underlayment before shingles. The flashing at chimneys and valleys. The completed roof from several angles. Either yours or the contractor's.

What voids a roof warranty

Most roof warranties are conditional, and the conditions are not hidden — they are simply not read. Common conditions that void otherwise valid warranties:

  • Failure to register the warranty within the manufacturer's window. Often 30 to 90 days. The product is still covered for basic defects, but extended terms (length, transferability) frequently do not apply.
  • Installation by an uncertified contractor. Extended manufacturer warranties usually require installation by a contractor in the manufacturer's certification program.
  • Subsequent work by uncertified parties. A repair by a different contractor can void the original warranty on the work that was repaired — or, in some cases, the surrounding sections.
  • Failure to perform required inspections. Some extended warranties require annual or biennial inspection records. No records, no claim.
  • Modifications without manufacturer approval. Solar panel installations are the most common example. Penetrations made by a third party can shift liability and void coverage.
  • Ventilation that does not meet specification. Frequently overlooked. If the attic ventilation doesn't meet the manufacturer's spec, premature shingle failure may not be covered.

None of these are uncommon. None of them are unreasonable. Most homeowners just never read the warranty.

A warranty you can claim is the one you've kept the receipts for. The rest is decorative.

The records to maintain after installation

The roof is rare among home systems in that it usually requires very little of you between installation and replacement. But "very little" is not "nothing." A small set of records, maintained calmly over time, preserves the full value of the warranty for its full term.

Annual visual inspection

Twice a year — typically after winter and after a major storm season — walk the perimeter and look at the roof from the ground. Note any obvious damage: missing shingles, displaced flashing, visible debris in valleys, sagging gutters. A short note with the date is enough. If something looks wrong, escalate to a professional inspection.

Storm event documentation

After any significant weather event — hail, high winds, falling debris — photograph the roof from the ground that day. Date the photos. This documentation is what insurance claims and warranty disputes hinge on, and it's nearly impossible to recreate after the fact.

Any work touching the roof

Any repair, modification, or third-party work on the roof becomes part of the roof's record. Skylight installation. Satellite dish removal. Gutter replacement. Solar panel installation. The contractor, scope, date, and any warranty implications for the existing roof.

Manufacturer inspections, if your warranty requires them

Read the warranty document at installation. If it specifies inspections, schedule them. Keep the inspection reports with the warranty document, not separately.

If your roof was installed before you owned the home

Most homeowners inherit a roof they didn't install. The records often arrive incomplete or not at all. The work to reconstruct them is doable.

  • Check county permit records. A roof replacement usually requires a permit. The permit will date the installation and often name the contractor.
  • Look at the closing documents. If the roof was replaced shortly before sale, the warranty may have been transferred (manufacturer warranties usually are transferable; workmanship warranties usually are not). The seller's disclosure should reference any major work.
  • Inspect the shingles for a brand stamp. A spare bundle in the garage or the back of a closet is a common find. The brand stamp will identify the product line and date code.
  • Contact the previous owner if possible. A short email asking for the contractor's information is often productive. Most prior owners want to be helpful.
  • Photograph what you can verify, accept what you can't. A roof with a known partial history is dramatically more credible than a roof with no history at all.

Why this matters at the moment of sale

A buyer's first question about a roof is "how old is it?" The second is "is it under warranty, and is the warranty transferable?" The third, increasingly, is "can I see the records?"

A complete roof record changes the conversation. It signals a well-kept home. It supports the seller's asking price. It removes a category of buyer concern that otherwise becomes a negotiating wedge. And it is one of the small differences that compresses the timeline between listing and closing.
