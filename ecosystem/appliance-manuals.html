Appliance manuals and what they're really for.

Most homeowner drawers contain the wrong half of the appliance record. The paper booklet is rarely the thing that matters. The model number, serial number, install date, and warranty terms almost always are.

Category Systems
Reading time 5 min
Last reviewed May 2026

Open the drawer. Count the manuals. There's a fair chance more than half of them are for appliances you no longer own. The other half are for appliances whose model numbers you cannot find when you actually need them, because the manual is in a drawer and the model number is on the back of the appliance.

This page is the decision tree. Two minutes per appliance, once, replaces every paper manual you've been holding onto.

What an appliance record actually needs

Per appliance

The five things to capture, once

Brand and model number.From the data plate. Photograph it directly. Don't transcribe — photograph.
Serial number.Same data plate. The serial encodes manufacture date and is what warranty claims reference.
Date of installation or purchase.If you remember it, capture it. If you don't, the serial number is often decodeable into a manufacture date.
Warranty terms.Length, what it covers, whether it's been registered. Almost always available on the manufacturer's website.
Receipt, if you have one.A photograph of the original receipt is what some warranty claims hinge on.

That's the entire record. Five items per appliance. The booklet itself is not on the list.

What the paper manual actually does

A paper manual contains four things: setup instructions, basic operation, troubleshooting, and specifications. Three of the four are available on the manufacturer's website, often as a downloadable PDF identical to the paper booklet, retrievable by model number in about ten seconds.

The exception — setup instructions — only matters at installation, which has typically already happened. The manual's job, in other words, is essentially done after the first week of ownership.

What does remain useful from a manual is rarely the manual itself: it's the small notes you sometimes find written inside the front cover by a previous owner. "Installed by Sears, March 2014, $89 delivery." Those notes are records. The booklet around them is not.

The manuals to keep are the manuals with notes inside them. The rest are reference material the internet keeps for you.

The decision tree

For every appliance in your home:

  1. Find the data plate. Usually back, side, bottom, inside door, or behind the kick plate.
  2. Photograph it. Both the model number and the serial number, clearly visible.
  3. Note the install or purchase date if you remember it.
  4. If there's an active warranty, locate the warranty document and the registration confirmation. If it's been registered. If not, decide whether it can still be registered (often within a window from purchase) or whether the window has passed.
  5. The paper manual can go.

For appliances you no longer own: the manual can go without further consideration.

The appliances worth treating with more care

A handful of major appliances justify a slightly longer record than the five items above. They are the ones with high replacement cost, long expected lifespans, or warranty terms that depend on documentation.

Refrigerator

Long-lifespan, high replacement cost, often with manufacturer warranties on the sealed system that extend beyond the general parts warranty. Worth recording maintenance: condenser coil cleanings, ice maker repairs, gasket replacements.

Range, oven, and cooktop

For gas units, the installation often required a permit. The permit, the installer, and the gas line connection are worth capturing.

Dishwasher

Surprisingly short average lifespan, frequent leaks, often the source of insurance claims. The install date and any leak history are worth recording.

Washing machine and dryer

Worth recording the install date, the water connection condition, and the dryer vent cleanout history. The dryer vent in particular is a fire-safety record worth keeping.

HVAC equipment, water heater, and similar permanent systems

These are not "appliances" in the casual sense, but they share the same data-plate-and-warranty structure. They get their own pages in this ecosystem — see the related links below.

What to do with appliance records when you sell

An appliance with a complete record — model, serial, install date, warranty, service history — is worth more to a buyer than the same appliance without one. Not by a lot, but measurably. The buyer who knows the dishwasher is six years old and under warranty doesn't ask for a credit on it. The buyer who doesn't know does.

At closing, the appliance records can be handed off cleanly. The next owner inherits the model numbers, the warranty positions, and the maintenance history — all of which they would otherwise spend their first year reconstructing.
