Why homeowners lose property history.

Property history disappears at every home, in every era, for the same set of reasons. It is not a failure of care. It is a failure of structure. Once you can see the structure, the loss is preventable for the first time.

Category Foundations
Reading time 9 min
Last reviewed May 2026

A home contains, at any given moment, an enormous amount of information about itself: when each thing was installed, who installed it, what was done to it, what was paid for it, what was promised about it, what's behind every finished surface, what's expected of every system. Most of this information is held in the head of the current owner, in a drawer of paper, in an email account, or in the memory of contractors who passed through years ago.

At the moment of sale — or death, or transfer of any kind — most of it disappears. The next owner begins again. They will accumulate their own version over years. When they leave, most of theirs will disappear too. The cycle repeats indefinitely, at every home, in every era.

This is not because homeowners are careless. It is because the structures that would hold the information don't exist. This page is about why.

The first reason: there is no place

A home record has no canonical place to live. The receipts go in a drawer. The warranties go in an email. The photographs go on a phone. The permits go to the county. The contractor's information goes on a business card. The maintenance schedule lives in the homeowner's head. Each of these locations is reasonable on its own. None of them connect to each other. None of them survive the homeowner.

The drawer is the closest most homes come to a record, and the drawer is not a record. It is the absence of one.

The second reason: the structure is invisible

Most homeowners do not have a mental model of what a complete home record looks like. They have never seen one. The category lacks a reference image. A homeowner trying to "organize their records" is trying to assemble a thing they have never been shown.

By contrast: most homeowners have a clear mental model of what a financial record looks like, because they've seen them at the bank, at the accountant, on their tax return. They have a clear model of what a medical record looks like, because they've seen them at the doctor. Home records have no equivalent. The mental model has to be built from scratch by every homeowner who attempts it, which is why most don't.

You can only organize a thing you've seen organized. Most homeowners have never seen a complete home record.

The third reason: the records arrive in incompatible forms

A homeowner record, if it existed, would draw from at least a dozen separate streams: closing documents, contractor invoices, manufacturer warranties, county permits, insurance policies, service receipts, maintenance reminders, model and serial numbers, before-and-after photographs, hand-written notes. Each stream arrives in a different format, from a different source, on a different schedule, often years apart.

Asking a homeowner to consolidate these into a single coherent record is asking them to build, in their personal time, the kind of integration project that organizations dedicate full-time roles to. Most homeowners try once, get partway, and let the project drift. The drift is rational. The task, as it's currently structured, is genuinely too large.

The fourth reason: the records move with the people, not the home

Almost everything that should be a property record is, in practice, an owner record. The receipts are in the owner's drawer. The contractor's information is in the owner's email. The warranty is registered to the owner's name. The photographs are on the owner's phone. The mental model is in the owner's head.

When the owner leaves, all of it leaves with them. The new owner does not inherit the records, because the records were never attached to the property. They were attached to a person — a person who is no longer involved.

This is the most quietly important structural problem in homeownership. The home itself is the most permanent asset most people will ever own. The records of that home are among the most transient.

The fifth reason: most of the knowledge was never written down

The records that exist on paper or in email are only a fraction of what a homeowner knows. The bulk of homeowner knowledge is tacit: which breaker controls which outlet, which window sticks in humid weather, where the main water shutoff is, what to do when the sump pump alarm sounds, which contractor was good and which one to avoid. None of this is captured in a document. All of it is real knowledge, and all of it dies at sale.

Long-term owners accumulate enormous amounts of this knowledge. The home becomes legible to them in ways no document could fully capture. When they leave, the home becomes opaque again to its next owner, who will spend years rebuilding the same understanding from scratch.

This is the part of property history that's hardest to preserve and most valuable to preserve. It is also the part that has, historically, had no place to live.

The sixth reason: the moment of transfer is the worst moment to organize

If property history were ever going to be consolidated and transferred, the natural moment would be the closing. In practice, the closing is the worst possible moment to do this work. The seller is consumed by the move and the transaction. The buyer is consumed by financing, inspections, and the dozens of decisions of taking possession. Neither party has the time, the attention, or the structure to produce or receive a complete record.

What gets transferred at most closings is what fits in a thin folder. Warranties on appliances, sometimes. The pool manual, occasionally. A scribbled list of who plows the driveway, maybe. The rest of the history — the work of decades — is left behind in the seller's head or recycled with the moving boxes.

The seventh reason: there's no obvious party to fix this

If you ask whose job it is to maintain a property's history, no one has a clean answer. Not the realtor — their relationship ends at closing. Not the inspector — they see the home for a few hours. Not the contractor — their record is for their work, not the home's. Not the insurer — their record is for their policy, not the property. Not the homeowner — they've never been shown how, and they have everything else to do.

In the absence of an obvious party, the responsibility falls implicitly on the homeowner — who reasonably treats it as a low-priority background task and rarely gets it done at the standard it deserves. The result is the present state of homeownership: enormous amounts of valuable information generated, almost none of it preserved.

What changes when the structure exists

Each of the seven reasons above is a structural problem, not a personal failure. Once a homeowner can see that the loss is structural, the loss becomes preventable. The drawer is not the place. The records don't have to live in incompatible forms. The history can be attached to the property rather than to the person. The tacit knowledge can be written down in small acts over time. The moment of transfer can be calm.

This is what Kodu exists for. The structural problem isn't a homeowner problem to solve — it's an infrastructure problem to solve, once, properly. The homeowner's job, in the right structure, becomes much smaller: capture, occasionally, calmly, in three-minute increments. The record builds itself from those small acts. Continuity becomes the default rather than the exception.

The home you live in does not have to be the same home that loses its history when you leave it. That is the entire premise.
