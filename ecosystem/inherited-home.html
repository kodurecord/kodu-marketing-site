Inheriting a home without records.

An inherited home arrives with partial information at the worst possible moment to absorb it. The work of understanding it can wait. Here is what to do calmly, in the order that protects both the home and the time you need to grieve.

Category Inheritance
Reading time 7 min
Last reviewed May 2026

Inheriting a home is unlike any other kind of homeownership. The transaction was not a transaction. There was no closing where someone walked you through the property. The previous owner is often unreachable in the most literal sense, and the questions you would have asked them are the questions you now have to answer alone.

This page is not urgent. None of what follows needs to happen this week, or this month. It is meant to be read once, and returned to over the year or two it usually takes to understand an inherited home properly.

What you've actually inherited

You've inherited a structure, the contents that came with it, the records that survive (probably partial), the systems and their unknown lifespans, the obligations that come with the property (taxes, insurance, any HOA), and — most importantly and least visibly — the knowledge that lived in the previous owner's head about how the house worked.

The last item is the one that's hardest to reconstruct and most worth trying. The breaker that controls the garage outlet. The window that sticks. The valve in the basement that has to be turned a particular way. Some of this can be inferred from the house itself. Some can be reconstructed from family members or neighbors who knew the home. Some is simply gone.

You inherited the house. You did not inherit the manual.

The first 30 days: only the essentials

In the first month, only a few things matter. Everything else can wait.

First month

What actually has to happen quickly

Secure the home.Change the exterior locks. Verify the alarm system if there is one. Note any spare keys you've found and consider their distribution.
Locate utility shutoffs.Main water, gas, electrical panel. Photograph each. You should be able to find them in the dark.
Update insurance.Most inherited homes need a policy change, often immediately. Standard homeowner coverage may not apply during the transition.
Check the basics.Water heater functioning, HVAC functioning, no obvious leaks, no obvious electrical issues, no obvious roof issues. A walk-through is enough.
Confirm utilities are in your name.A surprise utility shutoff during the transition is a common and avoidable problem.

That's the first month. Everything else, including the work of reconstruction, can begin when you're ready.

What records to look for

Most homes have records somewhere. They are often less organized than the previous owner imagined, but they're usually present in some form. Common locations:

  • The filing cabinet or home office. Closing documents, insurance, recent receipts. Often the most organized portion.
  • A drawer in the kitchen. Appliance manuals, contractor business cards, takeout menus mixed in with everything else.
  • The basement, garage, or attic. Older records, original house documents, project receipts from earlier decades. Frequently in a banker's box, sometimes labeled, sometimes not.
  • A safe or safe deposit box. Title documents, surveys, anything the previous owner considered irreplaceable.
  • The previous owner's email and computer. If accessible. Many warranty registrations, contractor communications, and receipts live only in email.
  • A neighbor or longtime friend who knew the house. Often the best source for what's been done over the years.

What can be reconstructed

Even with very little starting material, a substantial portion of an inherited home's history can be recovered. The work is slow, often spread over months, and worth the effort.

From county records

Permit history is almost always available, sometimes going back decades. Roof replacements, additions, HVAC installations, electrical work — all are likely to appear if permits were pulled. Some counties have online portals; others require a visit or a phone call.

From the property itself

Homes carry visible evidence of their history. The date code on a furnace label. The brand stamp on a shingle. The age of the electrical panel. The label on a water heater. A patient walkthrough — ideally with a notebook — reveals a great deal.

From service providers

If the previous owner used a particular HVAC company, plumber, or pest service, those companies often have years of records on file. A short phone call is often enough to receive a copy of past visits.

From neighbors

Long-term neighbors frequently remember when the roof was replaced, when the addition went on, who did the work. Their memory is informal but often accurate within a year or two.

From family

If the inheritance is from family, conversations with relatives about the home are often the most fruitful source. A short, recorded conversation about "what do you remember about this house" can produce more record than weeks of searching.

What to capture before it's gone

One narrow window is worth treating with urgency. Inside the home, during the first months, before anything has been moved or discarded, there is information available that won't survive a cleanout.

  • The position of every item the previous owner placed deliberately. The location of the toolbox. The breaker labels in the panel, even if hand-written. The contents of the kitchen junk drawer. Photograph all of it before reorganizing.
  • Notes the previous owner left for themselves. A scribbled date on the back of a water heater. A handwritten "filter changed" log on the side of a furnace. These are records.
  • Marked maps or sketches. Some homeowners leave behind a hand-drawn diagram of the property with annotations. If you find one, treat it as a primary document.

The pace that works

Inherited homes are not understood in a weekend. They are understood across seasons. The first heating season teaches you something about the heat. The first heavy rain teaches you something about the drainage. The first hot week teaches you something about the cooling. The first winter teaches you which windows leak.

This is the right pace. The pressure to know everything immediately is the pressure to make hasty decisions. The slower pace is the one that produces understanding.

Building a record from where you are

Eventually — at the right pace, in your own time — the inherited home becomes a documented home. The reconstruction is partial. Some history remains in the head of the family member who is no longer here. Some is recovered. Most is recoverable. The portion that isn't can be noted as such, and the record can move forward from this generation rather than being abandoned again.

The next time the home transfers, the record won't restart from zero. That is the meaning of continuity.
