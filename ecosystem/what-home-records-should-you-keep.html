Home Records

What home records should you keep?

Most homeowners hold onto too much of the wrong things and too little of the right things. The categories that matter are smaller than you'd think — and the ones to let go of are larger than you'd expect.

Category Records
Reading time 7 min
Last reviewed May 2026

If you've ever opened the drawer where you keep "house stuff" and felt a small wave of guilt, you're in the right place. The drawer is not the problem. The absence of a system for the drawer is the problem.

This guide names the four categories of records that matter for a home, what belongs in each one, and what you can safely recycle this afternoon. The categories aren't ours — they're the structure that long-term, well-organized homeowners arrive at on their own, usually after years of trial and error. Knowing them up front saves the years.

A home record is the only document in your house worth more the longer it exists.

The four categories of a home record

Everything worth keeping for a home falls into one of four buckets. If you can place a document in one of them, it's worth keeping. If you can't, it probably isn't.

1. The blueprint

Anything that describes the physical structure of your home and what's in it. Floor plans. Surveys. Permits. Architectural drawings. As-built diagrams from renovations. Photographs of the inside of walls before the drywall went back up. The blueprint answers the question: what is here, and where is it?

2. The vault

The proof. Warranties, manuals, closing documents, inspection reports, receipts for major work, model and serial numbers, contractor information. The vault answers: can I prove this happened, and can I find the people who did it?

3. The timeline

The story. When the roof was replaced. When the HVAC was serviced. When the kitchen was redone. When the panel was upgraded. The timeline answers: what has happened to this house, in what order, and when?

4. The guidance

The forward look. What's aging out, what's under warranty, what's due, what's next. Guidance is rarely written down — most homeowners carry it in their heads — and that's exactly why it disappears the moment a home changes hands. Guidance answers: what should the next person know about this house?

What belongs in each category

Here's the working list. Use it as a checklist for what to keep, what to find, and what to let go of.

Blueprint — the physical record

What describes the house itself

Survey or plot plan.Usually in your closing documents. Worth scanning and storing properly.
Floor plan, if you have one.Many homes don't. If yours doesn't, it's worth sketching one once.
Permits and their inspection reports.For your home and any work done while you've owned it. County records can fill in earlier permits.
Behind-the-wall photographs from any renovation.These are some of the most valuable records you'll ever own. They're also nearly impossible to recreate.
Locations of shutoffs.Main water, gas, electrical. A single photograph of each, with a note.
Vault — the proof

What gives you standing

Closing documents.The full packet from when you bought the home. Yes, all of it. It's smaller than you think.
Warranties for major systems and appliances.Roof, HVAC, water heater, windows, major appliances. The warranty document, the registration confirmation, and the original receipt.
Model and serial numbers.Photographs of the data plates on every major appliance and system. Two minutes per appliance, once.
Receipts for any work over a few hundred dollars.For warranty claims, insurance, and resale. Smaller receipts can usually go.
Inspection reports.Yours from purchase, any annual inspections (HVAC, roof, pest), and the buyer's inspection if you sell.
Contractor information.Names, license numbers, phone numbers, scope of work. The contractor's records are not yours — keep your own.
Timeline — the history

What records when

Install dates of major systems.Roof, HVAC, water heater, electrical panel, windows. Even approximate dates help.
Service visits.When, who, what they did, what it cost. The most commonly lost record category in any home.
Repairs.Plumbing, electrical, structural, exterior. With dates and contractors.
Renovations and improvements.With permits where applicable, photographs throughout, and final cost.
Guidance — the forward look

What you'd want the next person to know

Quirks of the house.The breaker that controls the garage outlet. The window that sticks. The valve that has to be turned a particular way.
Maintenance you do annually.What you check, when, and why.
Anything that's been deferred.A known repair you haven't done yet. A warranty you haven't claimed.

What you can let go of

Decluttering is half the battle, and most homeowners err on the side of keeping. Here is permission to discard.

  • Paper manuals for appliances you still own — almost every manual is available online by model number. Keep the model and serial, recycle the booklet.
  • Manuals for appliances you no longer own — this is the largest category in most homeowner drawers. All of it can go.
  • Receipts under a few hundred dollars — unless they support an active warranty.
  • Old contractor business cards — capture the name and phone in your record and recycle the card.
  • Junk mail with "important" stamped on it — usually a marketing tactic, rarely a record.
  • Closing documents from a home you no longer own — typically safe to discard after seven years, unless your accountant says otherwise.

The drawer is not a record. The drawer is the absence of one.

How long to keep what you keep

For most homeowner records, the answer is as long as you own the home, and then transfer them to the next owner. The records belong to the home, not to you. A few specifics:

  • Warranties — for the full warranty term. Don't discard them at expiration; some warranty disputes are resolved months after.
  • Permits and inspections — indefinitely. These are part of the home's permanent record.
  • Renovation documentation — indefinitely. Photographs from inside walls become more valuable over decades, not less.
  • Service receipts — at minimum, for the life of the system. Ideally, indefinitely. Service history is what gives a major repair its credibility at sale.
  • Tax-related improvement documentation — per your accountant's guidance, usually at least seven years past sale.

The hardest record to keep, and the most important

Of the four categories, guidance is the one most homeowners never write down. The quirks, the order-of-operations, the things the previous owner would have told you over coffee if they'd thought to. Guidance is the record that dies most cleanly at the moment of sale, and the one the next owner most wishes had survived.

The good news: guidance doesn't have to be elaborate. A short note for each room, a list of quirks, a single annotated photograph of the breaker panel — these small acts of writing things down outlast almost everything else in the record.

Where to keep all of this

The honest answer: not in a drawer. Not in a binder that lives in a basement. Not in a Dropbox folder you'll forget about. The right place for a home record is somewhere that's organized around the home itself — by room, by system, by project, by date — not by what shape the paper happened to come in.
