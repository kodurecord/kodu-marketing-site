# Content Strategy

This document governs what the Kodu marketing site publishes, why it publishes it, and the bar every page must clear before it ships. It is the parent document for the rest of the `docs/` folder. When another document conflicts with this one, this one wins.

## What Kodu is

Kodu is a private homeowner property record platform — the living record of a single property, organized around the actual anatomy and lifecycle of that property.

Kodu is **not**:

- a public records or property-lookup portal
- a generic file storage or document bin
- a contractor CRM or job-management tool
- a marketplace or lead-generation service
- a generic SaaS dashboard

The marketing site exists to explain that distinction clearly, calmly, and without hype. Every piece of content either teaches a homeowner something true and useful, or it does not belong here.

## The governing principle: teach before selling

The ecosystem earns trust by being genuinely useful before it ever asks for anything. A homeowner should be able to read most of the site, learn something real about caring for and documenting their property, and leave better off even if they never sign up.

Selling is a consequence of usefulness, not a replacement for it. We do not gate the teaching behind a signup. We do not bait with a question and answer it only after a form. The value is on the page.

## The category Kodu is creating

Kodu is creating and teaching a new category:

**The Private Homeowner Property Record.**

This category is not public property records, generic cloud storage, a contractor
portal, or a marketplace. It is the private, homeowner-controlled record of a
single property: what happened, what changed, what proof exists, who can see it,
and what can be carried forward to a future owner.

The ecosystem should make this category feel obvious by the time Kodu is
introduced. A reader should first understand the homeowner problem, then the
need for a private property record, and only then why Kodu is the natural place
for that record to live.

Do not explain the whole product on every page. Each page teaches one chapter
of the category and points to the next useful chapter.

## Connected learning journey

Kodu pages are not isolated SEO articles. They are connected chapters in a
homeowner learning journey.

Every page must:

- Answer one real homeowner question.
- Teach one clear idea well.
- Reinforce one or more of the five pillars.
- Link naturally to the next chapter.
- Use Kodu only at the level the reader is ready for.

The question is the page's boundary. If a section starts answering a different
question, either link to the canonical page for that question or create a
separate brief. The page should not become a complete explanation of Kodu unless
its actual question is product-specific.

## The five reinforcement pillars

Every page must reinforce at least one — ideally several — of these five ideas. They are the spine of the entire ecosystem.

1. **Property memory** — A home accumulates a history: what was built, repaired, replaced, paid for, and warrantied. That history is normally scattered or lost. Kodu keeps it intact.
2. **Proof** — Records are only valuable if they can be trusted and retrieved. Receipts, warranties, permits, and photos become evidence a homeowner can actually use.
3. **Privacy** — The record is private by default. It is not searchable by the public, not shared without intent, and not exposed by accident.
4. **Homeowner control** — The homeowner owns and directs their record. They decide what is captured, who can see it, and how it is organized.
5. **Transfer readiness** — A well-kept record is one of the most valuable things a homeowner can hand to the next owner. The record is built from day one to be transferable.

If a draft page reinforces none of these, it is off-strategy and should be reworked or cut.

## The page-level test

Before any page is written, it must answer a single real homeowner question — a question a real person would actually type or ask aloud. Examples of the *shape* of valid questions:

- "Where do I keep my appliance warranties so I can actually find them later?"
- "What records should I have before I sell my house?"
- "How do I prove a repair was done if I ever need to?"
- "What should a new homeowner document first?"

The question goes at the top of the content brief. The page is judged on whether it answers that question well. A page that answers no clear question, or quietly answers a marketing question instead of a homeowner question, fails the test.

## Foundation sequence

The first learning path teaches the category before it asks for commitment.
These pages should be built and linked as a sequence.

| Page | Target homeowner question | Page purpose | Primary audience | Emotional goal | Next-page CTA | Kodu connection level |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| What Is a Home Property Record? | What is a home property record, and why should I keep one? | Define the category in homeowner language. | New and existing homeowners who know their records are scattered. | Recognition: "My home does have a record." | Learn why every home has a history | Light: Kodu is named as a private place for the record. |
| Every Home Has a Story | What does it mean that my home has a history? | Show that repairs, warranties, photos, projects, and decisions form a living story. | Homeowners beginning to think beyond folders and receipts. | Connection: "The work I do on my home matters over time." | See why homeowners lose that story | Very light: Kodu appears as one way to preserve the story. |
| Why Homeowners Lose Their Property History | Why is it so easy to lose track of home records? | Explain the scattered-record problem without fear. | Homeowners with email, paper, photos, and memory spread across places. | Relief: "The problem is normal and solvable." | So where should all of this live? | Moderate: Kodu is introduced as a better organizing model. |
| How Kodu Creates Your Property's Memory | How does Kodu help my home keep its memory? | Explain the product model through property memory, proof, privacy, and continuity. | Qualified homeowners who understand the problem and want a system. | Confidence: "There is a structure for this." | Explore inside a Kodu property record | Strong: Kodu's model becomes the answer. |

This sequence is the canonical category introduction. Later pages should link
into it rather than re-explaining the whole idea.

## CTA philosophy

Do not default every page to "Sign up."

Use progression CTAs that match the reader's stage:

- "Learn why every home has a history"
- "See why homeowners lose that story"
- "So where should all of this live?"
- "See how Kodu creates your property's memory"
- "Explore inside a Kodu property record"

Request-access CTAs should appear calmly and naturally, usually after the page
has taught the problem and shown why a private property record matters. Earlier
pages may include a quiet request-access path, but their main CTA should usually
be the next learning chapter.

## Visual storytelling rule

Each page should feel like part of one structured education experience, not a
text article with decorative images added afterward.

Use visual storytelling to make the homeowner problem concrete:

- Hero image or product-inspired visual.
- Proof, document, receipt, warranty, photo, or record visual.
- Checklist, folder, timeline, property profile, or record example.
- Video placeholder when a short explanation would help.
- Related next-chapter cards that show where the reader should go next.

Visuals should support the page's single question. Do not add random stock-photo
imagery, browser chrome, development badges, or app mockups that do not reflect
the real Kodu product language. Kodu app-inspired visuals should use top
navigation only unless the real product uses the sidebar pattern shown.

## Content types

The ecosystem uses a small, deliberate set of content types. We do not invent new formats casually; format sprawl is a form of feature sprawl.

- **Guides** — Practical, evergreen explanations of how to document or care for a part of the property (warranties, permits, roof history, appliance records, etc.).
- **Concept pages** — Explain a Kodu idea (property memory, transfer readiness, private-by-default) in homeowner terms.
- **Comparisons** — Honest explanations of how a property record differs from the things people confuse it with (a folder of PDFs, a public records search, a contractor's files). Comparisons describe; they do not disparage.
- **Reference / glossary** — Short, factual definitions of terms a homeowner encounters.
- **Product pages** — Describe what Kodu does. These still teach and still answer a real question; they are not exempt from the standards here.

## Page expansion libraries

Future ecosystem pages should grow from these libraries. Each library must stay
connected to the foundation sequence and the five pillars.

- **Property Records** - definitions, proof, privacy, transfer readiness, and
  comparisons to folders, public records, and generic storage.
- **Maintenance** - what to track, what proof to keep, and how records help
  future decisions.
- **Systems** - HVAC, plumbing, electrical, roof, water heater, appliances,
  exterior systems, drainage, irrigation, and safety systems.
- **Projects** - remodels, repairs, improvements, permits, budgets, photos,
  plans, and project timelines.
- **Insurance** - proof, photos, receipts, maintenance records, and claim-ready
  documentation without promising outcomes.
- **Buying & Selling** - transfer readiness, inherited homes, new-owner setup,
  seller preparation, and records a future owner should inherit.
- **Homeowner Knowledge** - plain-language explainers for homeowner decisions,
  terms, documents, and maintenance concepts.
- **Kodu Education** - product-specific pages that explain how Kodu organizes
  the property record after the homeowner understands the problem.

System pages must explain:

- How the system works in homeowner language.
- What homeowners should know.
- What records matter.
- What proof should be saved.
- What future owners should inherit.

These pages should not become contractor guides. They should help homeowners
understand, document, and preserve the record of their own property.

## What we do not publish

- Fear-based or alarmist content ("your home is at risk if you don't…").
- Unsupported claims about outcomes, savings, insurance results, or home value.
- Hype, superlatives, or invented urgency.
- Contractor or industry jargon presented as if homeowners should already know it.
- Content that implies Kodu is a public-records search, a marketplace, or generic storage.
- "Thin" pages written only to rank, with no real answer for a real person.
- Claims about features that do not exist or are not yet shipped.

## Editorial governance

- Every content brief names the homeowner question and the pillar(s) it reinforces before drafting begins.
- Drafts are reviewed against this document, the writing style guide, and the SEO/AI strategy.
- Product claims must be verifiable against the actual product. If a claim cannot be supported, it is removed, not softened.
- Quality is preferred over volume. A smaller number of excellent, trustworthy pages serves the brand better than a large number of shallow ones.
